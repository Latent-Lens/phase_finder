Short version: PhaseFinder measures the cell cycle the same way the rest of the field does — we checked our math, step by step, against every open recipe we could find and against FlowJo's own written instructions. This page explains that in plain language.

1. What this page answers

One fair question about any analysis tool is: “How do I know it's doing the math right, and not just making up a nice-looking number?” This page answers that for PhaseFinder's cell-cycle fitting. It does not ask “how accurate is it on real samples” — that's a separate page, How accurate is it on real samples?

2. What the tool is actually doing

When a cell is getting ready to divide, it copies its DNA. So at any moment a cell is in one of three situations:

  • G1 — one copy of DNA (hasn't started copying yet).
  • S — busy copying, so somewhere between one and two copies.
  • G2/M — two full copies, about to split.

If you stain the DNA and measure a whole population, you get a histogram with two humps: a tall one at “one copy” (G1) and a shorter one at “two copies” (G2/M), with a low bridge between them (the S cells, caught mid-copy). PhaseFinder's job is to figure out what fraction of cells sits under each part — how much G1, how much S, how much G2/M.

It does that by drawing a smooth mathematical curve through the histogram and reading the areas off it. The recipe for that curve has a name: Dean-Jett-Fox (after the scientists who worked it out in the 1970s–80s). The expensive programs most labs trust — FlowJo, ModFit — use the same recipe. The catch: their code is closed, so you can't just read it and check.

The one-sentence version

Two Gaussian “bell curves” for the G1 and G2/M humps, plus a gently curved ramp for the S bridge between them. Add up the areas → the phase percentages.

Since we can't read FlowJo's code, we lined our math up against the recipes we can read — and against FlowJo's own published description of how it works:

We also checked a package people often assume does this (RGLab's flowCore) and confirmed it has no cell-cycle model at all — it's plumbing for reading the data, not fitting it. Worth knowing, so nobody points at it as a reference it isn't.

4. Finding the two humps

Before you can fit a curve you have to find the two humps. Think of it like spotting the two tallest hills on a bumpy horizon and ignoring the little bushes. Every one of these tools — including ours — uses the same basic recipe:

  • Smooth the histogram a little so noise doesn't look like a hump.
  • Find the local high points that are tall enough to be real.
  • Throw away junk at the far-left edge (dead cells and debris pile up there).
  • Call the big one G1.
  • Expect the second hump at about double the DNA (because two copies) — and look for it there.

That “second hump at double” rule is the anchor everyone leans on, because it's simple biology: two copies is twice the DNA. PhaseFinder uses it exactly like the others. The only difference is that PhaseFinder does the search at several zoom levels at once and scores each candidate on more features, and it tells you how confident it is (a clear pair, a weak pair, or a guessed second hump) — where the simpler tools just take the two tallest and move on.

5. Drawing the curve

Once the humps are located, you build the curve. Here's the recipe, and how ours lines up:

Piece of the curveEveryone does…PhaseFinder
The two humps (G1, G2/M) Bell curves Same — and we add up each bell across the width of a histogram bar rather than just sampling its height, which is a touch more accurate for counts.
The S bridge between them A gently curved ramp (a quadratic), blurred a little to match how the peaks spread Same
How “good” a fit is scored Least-squares (a common approximation) The exact count-based measure (Poisson) — the statistically correct one for counting data.

The one real difference — and it's a good one

FlowJo pins down the two humps first, using only the clean outer side of each hump (where S cells aren't leaking in), and only then fits the S bridge. Our older approach fit everything at once, which occasionally let the flexible S bridge “swallow” a hump on lopsided samples and give a nonsense answer. PhaseFinder now uses FlowJo's peaks-first approach, which fixes that. So the difference we found wasn't a wrong formula — it was a smarter order of operations, and we adopted it.

6. The bottom line

  • Same method as everyone else. The way PhaseFinder finds the humps and draws the curve matches the independent open-source programs and FlowJo's own instructions.
  • A little more careful in places. More exact bell-curve math, the statistically correct count-based fit measure, multi-zoom hump-finding, and honest confidence labels.
  • The one gap we found, we closed. Fitting the humps first (FlowJo's way) instead of everything at once.
