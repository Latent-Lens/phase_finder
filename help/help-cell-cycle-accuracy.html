We ran PhaseFinder on 45 real samples that already had “official” answers from two trusted programs, and compared. Short version: PhaseFinder lands within about 5 percentage points of FlowJo, and is the closest match on 27 of 30 samples. The twist: the two trusted programs disagree with each other more than PhaseFinder disagrees with them.

1. What this page answers

This page is about accuracy on real data: when PhaseFinder says “42% G1, 18% S, 40% G2,” how close is that to what the established tools say? If you instead want to know whether the underlying method is done correctly, see Is the cell-cycle math done right?

2. How we tested it

We used a set of real (asynchronous yeast) samples where someone had already measured the cell-cycle fractions with professional tools — think of it as an answer key we didn't write:

  • 30 samples with answers from FlowJo (using its Dean-Jett-Fox model).
  • 15 of those also had answers from Flowreader (using its Watson model).

We ran every PhaseFinder model on all of them and measured the gap, in percentage points (pp), between our answer and the answer key — averaged across the three phases. Smaller is closer. A gap of “5 pp” means, on average, each phase percentage was about 5 points off (e.g. we said 40% where the key said 45%).

5. The important catch: the answer keys disagree with each other

For the 15 samples that had both a FlowJo answer and a Flowreader answer, we can compare the two answer keys directly. They disagree with each other by:

PhaseHow much FlowJo and Flowreader disagree (same samples)
G11.7 pp (they basically agree)
S14.1 pp (big disagreement)
G2/M7.6 pp

There is no single “true” answer

On the very same sample, FlowJo might say 27% S-phase and Flowreader might say 41% S-phase — a 14-point difference, from two respected tools, on identical data. They draw the line between S and G2 differently. So when PhaseFinder is “12 points off Flowreader,” most of that is really Flowreader disagreeing with FlowJo, not PhaseFinder being wrong. Our models agree with FlowJo to ~5 points and sit reasonably between the two references.

This is the real reason PhaseFinder offers more than one model, and why we ask you to pick one on purpose rather than letting the tool choose: which “official” number you want to reproduce depends on which tool your field or your collaborators use.

6. Where the peaks sit — and why we don’t nudge them to match

Everything above is about fractions (how much G1, S, G2). There is a second number worth understanding: where PhaseFinder puts the peaks on the DNA axis. On the same 30 samples, our G1 peak lands about 1.6% lower than FlowJo’s and our G2 peak about 3.2% lower. Both are well inside the tolerance we test against, but they are consistent enough to explain.

They are not two problems. They are one small difference plus one deliberate choice:

ComponentSizeWhat it is
G1 peak position −1.6% A peak-width difference, not a position error — see below
G2:G1 ratio −1.6% A deliberate scientific choice — see below
G2 peak position −3.2% Just the two above added together — G1 shifts, and the ratio shifts it again

The ratio: we let the data choose, and it says 1.98, not 2.00

A G2 cell has exactly twice the DNA of a G1 cell, so it is tempting to insist the G2 peak sit at exactly 2.00× the G1 peak. Real stained cells don’t quite do that. During G2/M the chromatin condenses, which makes it physically harder for the dye to reach the DNA, so G2/M cells glow slightly less than twice as bright. That is a real, documented effect, not a measurement artifact.

PhaseFinder fits the ratio freely and gets a median of 1.977 — right where that effect predicts. FlowJo’s reference numbers cluster on 2.009, essentially the textbook 2.00, because FlowJo can constrain the ratio during fitting. So the “disagreement” here is a difference of convention: we report what the sample did, they report it pinned to theory.

We will not tune this to match

Pulling our G2 peak up to 2.00× G1 would make our numbers look closer to FlowJo’s while making them less faithful to the sample. If you need FlowJo-convention numbers for a comparison, note the ~1.6% ratio difference and apply it — don’t expect the fit to reproduce it.

The G1 offset: our peaks are narrower, so they centre lower

The G1 difference turned out not to be about position at all. FlowJo reports G1 peaks roughly 1.5× wider than we fit (CV ~9.2 vs ~6.2), and real G1 peaks lean to the right — the tail on the high-DNA side is heavier. Fit a wider bell curve to a right-leaning peak and its centre is dragged up the heavy side; fit a narrower one and it settles lower, closer to the true summit.

We confirmed this directly: take our own histogram, keep everything the same, and only force FlowJo’s peak width — the gap closes by about three quarters on its own. Which width is the right one is an open question we are still measuring, so for now we report the narrower fit and tell you the offset exists rather than quietly closing it.

Practical upshot

If you are comparing peak channels with a FlowJo user, expect ours to run ~1.6% low at G1 and ~3.2% low at G2. If you are comparing phase fractions — what most people actually report — this barely matters, because both peaks move together.

7. So which model should I pick?

  • Want to match FlowJo (or ModFit) numbers? Use Dean-Jett-Fox. It's the same model family and matched FlowJo best on 27 of 30 samples.
  • Want to match Flowreader / a Watson-style number? Use Watson Classic. It's built to reproduce that style and matched Flowreader best on 10 of 15.
  • Not sure / no external reference to match? Dean-Jett-Fox is the sensible default — it's the most widely used cell-cycle model and the most thoroughly checked here.

Whichever you pick, PhaseFinder always shows you the fitted curve, a fit-quality score, and a warning if anything looked off — so you can see for yourself whether to trust the number, rather than the tool hiding it or deciding for you.
