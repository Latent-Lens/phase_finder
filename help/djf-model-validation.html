PhaseFinder computes cell-cycle phase fractions (G0/G1, S, G2/M) from a DNA-content histogram using a Dean-Jett-Fox (DJF) model and two Watson variants. Because the reference tools most labs trust — FlowJo and Flowreader — are closed source, this page records how PhaseFinder's math was checked against the open implementations that do exist, plus FlowJo's own published description of its algorithm. The short version: PhaseFinder follows the same canonical DJF formulation the field uses, and is more numerically careful on several points.

1. Purpose

Two questions motivated this review:

  • Are we modeling DJF the way everyone else does? A phase-fraction number is only trustworthy if the underlying model matches the accepted definition of the method, not a private reinterpretation of it.
  • Where PhaseFinder disagrees with FlowJo on real data, is that a formula bug or a fitting choice? The two have very different remedies.

The canonical method comes from Dean & Jett (1974) — two Gaussian peaks for G1 and G2/M with a broadened polynomial S-phase between them — and Fox (1980), which adds a Gaussian “wave” to the S-phase distribution. FlowJo, Flowreader, ModFit, and MultiCycle all descend from this. There is no single reference source code, so the check below triangulates across several independent implementations.

On flowCore

flowCore is frequently assumed to implement DJF because it is the foundational R flow-cytometry package. It does not: a full-text search of the repository finds no Dean-Jett, Fox, S-phase, or cell-cycle code. It provides flowFrame/ flowSet data structures, gating, transforms, and FCS reading only. No well-maintained open-source R package implements the full DJF S-phase model; the closest open code is Python (above).

3. What FlowJo documents

FlowJo's public documentation describes its univariate cell-cycle algorithm in enough detail to compare directly. The key procedural points:

  • The G1 and G2/M peaks are fit first, as Gaussians, by least squares.
  • Each peak is fit only over its clean flank — roughly −3σ to +1σ about the mean — so that S-phase counts leaking into the inner shoulder do not distort the peak width.
  • Peak width is characterized at 60% of maximum height.
  • Only after the peaks are pinned is the S-phase modeled, as a polynomial with a Fox Gaussian broadening term, over what remains between the peaks.

The important insight is procedural, not formulaic: FlowJo decouples peak estimation from S-phase estimation. That ordering is what keeps a G2-heavy sample from letting the S-phase term swallow the G2 peak. PhaseFinder's clean_flank peak-fit mode reproduces this ordering (see §6).

The broadening coupling is standard, not a bug

PhaseFinder ties S-phase broadening to the G1 CV. That coupling was initially suspected as a cause of disagreement with FlowJo, but the independent Vannier19 DJF does exactly the same thing. It is the standard Dean-Jett convention. The remaining FlowJo gap is a fitting-procedure difference, not a formula error — see below.

5. Peak calling, step by step

Every implementation — including the dedicated R peak-detection package — follows the same recipe: smooth the histogram, find local maxima above a prominence or height threshold, discard low-channel debris, take the dominant/leftmost peak as G1, and anchor G2 near twice the G1 channel.

PhaseFinder's peak caller is the most elaborate of the four — multi-scale detection, multi-feature pair scoring, and explicit confidence tiers — but it is built on the identical logic the others use, including the universal ≈2× G2 anchor.

6. Empirical agreement

Formula agreement was then checked against real data. PhaseFinder was run on an independent validation set of asynchronous yeast samples that carry external phase-fraction references: 30 samples with FlowJo DJF values and 15 of those also with Flowreader Watson values. All four model variants were scored against both reference sets.

The fitting-procedure fix, on representative samples

The clearest illustration of why fitting order matters. The original DJF joint fit — peaks and S-phase optimized together — degenerates on skewed samples, with S-phase either collapsing to 0% or ballooning to swallow a peak. Fitting the peaks from their clean flanks first (clean_flank mode) removes the degeneracy and tracks FlowJo closely. Values are G1 / S / G2 percentages.

The two reference tools disagree more than our models do

On the 15 samples that carry both references, FlowJo DJF and Flowreader Watson disagree with each other by a mean of 14.1 pp in S and 7.6 pp in G2 (they agree on G1 to 1.7 pp). For example 1468f is 22 / 27 / 46 by FlowJo DJF but 22 / 41 / 37 by Flowreader Watson — the same data, a 14-point difference in S. So the large S-phase “errors” in the Watson table are mostly the two tools partitioning the S/G2 boundary differently, not a PhaseFinder failure: our models track FlowJo DJF to ~5 pp, and Watson Classic bridges partway toward the Watson split. This is the practical case for offering both model families rather than one.

7. Conclusions

  • PhaseFinder models DJF the way the field does. Its peak model (area-normalized Gaussians, constant CV, ≈2× anchor), its S-phase model (broadened non-negative quadratic with broadening tied to the G1 CV), and its peak caller (smoothed local maxima, prominence threshold, debris rejection, G1-dominant, G2 at 2×) all match the independent open-source implementations.
  • On several points PhaseFinder is more careful: Gaussians integrated over bins rather than sampled at centers, position-dependent S-phase broadening by quadrature rather than a single averaged σ, a proper Poisson deviance objective, multi-scale peak detection with confidence tiers, and the actual Fox wave that the open-source “DJF” implementations omit.
  • Disagreement with FlowJo was a fitting choice, not a formula error. FlowJo pins the peaks from their clean flanks before modeling S-phase; PhaseFinder's clean_flank mode reproduces that ordering and removes the joint-fit degeneracy on skewed samples.
  • On real data, DJF clean-flank matches FlowJo DJF to ~5 pp per phase and is the closest model on 26 of 30 samples, while Watson Classic is the closest to the Flowreader Watson reference on 9 of 15. Because the two reference tools themselves differ by ~14 pp in S, offering both DJF and Watson model families — rather than forcing one — is the right design.

References: Dean P.N. & Jett J.H. (1974) J. Cell Biol. 60:523–527; Fox M.H. (1980) Cytometry 1:71–77; FlowJo cell-cycle documentation; open-source implementations cited in §2.
